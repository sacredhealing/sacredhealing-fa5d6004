The Saints of the Living Fire

Those Who Walked in Jah · The African Lineage · The Holy Ones of Ethiopia and the Desert

Before Europe had a church, Africa had saints. Before Rome wrote theology, Alexandria was teaching it. Before the West knew the name of Yeshua, Ethiopia had already built monasteries in His honour on the highest mountains of the continent. These are not distant figures in stained glass. These are 1-and-1 ancestors. These are the ones who burned so hot in the fire of Jah that the world could not contain them. Read their lives and recognise yourself.

✦ The Nine Saints of Ethiopia · The Founders · 5th–6th Century They Came from Across the World and Ethiopia Received Them · They Built the Monasteries · They Translated the Word

Know this first: Christianity did not come to Ethiopia from Europe. It never did and it never will. Ethiopia received the Gospel from the Ethiopian eunuch who met Philip on the road to Gaza — before Paul reached Europe, before Peter reached Rome, before any council had sat. The church was already burning on the mountains of Abyssinia. And then came the Nine — nine holy men from Syria, from Rome, from Antioch, from Constantinople — who heard the call of Jah and walked to Ethiopia and never left. They are the founders of the deepest Christian tradition that still lives on this earth. Give thanks.

Abba Aragawi — Za-Mikael — He Who the Serpent Carried

Abba Aragawi came to Ethiopia in the 5th century and looked up at the mountain of Debre Damo — a sheer cliff rising from the earth with no path, no stairs, no way up. And Jah sent a great serpent down from the top of the mountain and the serpent lifted Aragawi and carried him to the summit. There he built the monastery of Debre Damo — the oldest standing church in the world — a monastery that to this day can only be reached by rope, as Aragawi was reached by the serpent of Jah. The building still stands. The rope still hangs. The monks still climb. Jah does not abandon what He has established.

Abba Garima — Isaac — He Who Copied the Gospels in One Day

Abba Garima sat down one morning to copy the Four Gospels by hand. He asked Jah to stop the sun so he could finish before dark. And the sun stopped. And Abba Garima copied all four Gospels in one day. The Garima Gospels still exist. They have been tested and dated — between 390 and 570 AD — the oldest illustrated Christian manuscript in the world. Not in Rome. Not in Constantinople. In Ethiopia. In the monastery that bears his name. The word of Jah preserved in Africa before Europe could read.

Abba Pantalewon — He Who Stood

Abba Pantalewon came to the mountains of Tigray and found a rock. And he stood on that rock and he did not move. Not for days. Not for weeks. He stood in the presence of Jah on a stone until the stone became holy ground. No comfort. No cushion. No compromise. Just the fire of Jah and the decision to burn in it. That is the Nazarite vow lived in the body. That is what overstanding looks like when it becomes flesh.

Abba Guba — The Healer · He Who Came from Cilicia

Abba Guba left Cilicia and came to Ethiopia and went into the desert of Baraka. And in that desert he healed. He laid hands and fevers broke. He prayed and the broken were made whole. He founded the monastery of Madara. The people came from everywhere and he turned no one away. A healer in the desert — that is the image of Yeshua walking in Galilee. Abba Guba walked in Ethiopia. Same spirit. Same fire.

Saint Tekle Haymanot · 1215–1313 · The Tree of the Holy Spirit

His name means Tree of the Holy Spirit. And he was exactly that — rooted so deep in Jah that nothing could move him. Tekle Haymanot went into a cave in the mountains of Shewa and he stood in prayer. He stood for seven years. One leg. Seven years. And his other leg fell from his body — the flesh gave out before the spirit would — and he continued to stand on one leg for the remaining time. And Jah looked at this man standing on one leg in a cave for seven years and grew him wings. Six wings, it is said. And he flew. And he founded Debre Libanos — the greatest monastery in Ethiopia — which stands to this day as the spiritual heart of the nation. Haile Selassie 1 himself traced his lineage through this place. Tekle Haymanot is not mythology. He is the standard.

The declaration of Tekle Haymanot: "1 will not sit until 1 have stood before Jah. 1 will not rest until the work of the spirit is complete."

Saint Yared · 505–571 AD · He Who Received the Music from Heaven

Before Bob Marley. Before Nyahbinghi. Before the drum and the bass and the chant — there was Yared. Saint Yared of Ethiopia, born 505 AD, who went into the forest as a young man and watched three birds singing in a tree and heard in their song the music of the divine. And Jah gave him the entire system of Ethiopian Orthodox sacred music — the modes, the rhythms, the chants, the notation — in a single revelation. He sang before the Emperor Gebre Meskel and the Emperor was so transported by the music that his spear pierced Yared's foot and neither of them noticed until the song ended, for both were gone into Jah. Every chant that the Ethiopian deacon sings today, every drum that beats in every Orthodox church in Africa, traces back to this one man in a forest listening to three birds and hearing the voice of the Most High. Music is not entertainment. Music is revelation. Yared knew it first.

Saint Lalibela · 1162–1221 · The Emperor Who Built Jerusalem in Africa

Lalibela was an Ethiopian emperor who had a vision: he would build a New Jerusalem in Africa so that his people would never have to make the dangerous pilgrimage to the city that had been taken by the Crusaders. And Jah showed him how. He went into the rock — the living red volcanic rock of the mountains of Lasta — and he carved. Eleven churches. Carved from the living rock. Down into the earth and up from it simultaneously. The largest, Bete Medhane Alem — House of the Saviour of the World — is the largest monolithic church on earth. Carved from a single piece of rock. Tradition says the angels worked alongside the workers through the night, completing in darkness what the men could not finish in daylight. Lalibela stands today. Pilgrims walk through its trenches barefoot. The rock still bleeds red. Go and see it before you die.

Walatta Petros · 1592–1643 · The Ethiopian Warrior of Faith

When the Portuguese Jesuits came to Ethiopia with their Roman Catholicism and tried to replace the ancient Ethiopian Orthodox faith — the faith that had been burning in those mountains for twelve hundred years before Portugal was a nation — Walatta Petros stood up. She was a noblewoman. She left her husband, left her wealth, left her comfort, and organised the resistance. Community by community, monastery by monastery, she gathered the faithful and refused. She was captured. She was exiled. She came back. They exiled her again. She came back again. Three of her children died in infancy and she said it was Jah teaching her that her children were the spiritual community she was building, not the flesh. She died in 1643 and her disciples — women — wrote her biography in Ge'ez. It is the oldest known book-length biography of an African woman written by Africans. An African woman, defending African faith, against European religious colonisation, in the 17th century. The spirit of Walatta Petros is the spirit of every Rasta who has ever held the livity against the pressure of Babylon. She did it first.

Saint Gebre Menfes Qiddus · He Who Lived with the Lions

They say Gebre Menfes Qiddus lived 363 years. Whether the number is literal or figurative, what is certain is this: he walked into the wilderness and he stayed there. He ate nothing that had been harmed. He lived on plants and on prayer. The lions came to him and he did not run. The leopards came and he blessed them. The wild animals of the Ethiopian highlands surrounded this holy man and in his presence they became peaceful — predator and prey resting together — because in the field of a truly purified consciousness even the animal kingdom reflects the peace of Zion. Ital livity in its most extreme form. The body so purified, the aura so clear, that creation itself reorganises around you. That is what Gebre Menfes Qiddus showed. That is the promise at the end of the Ital path.

Saint Frumentius · Abba Salama · The Light of Peace · 4th Century

A Phoenician merchant ship was attacked off the coast of Ethiopia. Two boys survived — Frumentius and his brother Aedesius. They were taken to the court of the Ethiopian king in Aksum. Frumentius rose to become the royal secretary and treasurer. When the king died he helped the queen rule the kingdom. And in that position — at the heart of the most powerful African empire of the ancient world — he began to share the Gospel. When his work was done he went to Alexandria and asked Athanasius to send a bishop to Ethiopia. Athanasius looked at Frumentius and said: who better to send than you? He consecrated Frumentius as the first Bishop of Ethiopia and sent him back. Abba Salama — Father of Peace — returned to the land that had received him as a shipwrecked orphan boy and became the spiritual father of a nation. Ethiopia was Christian before Rome was Christian. Remember that.

Saint Anthony the Great · 251–356 AD · The Father of All Who Seek Jah in Silence

Anthony was Egyptian. Born in Coma, in the Nile delta. He heard one day in the church the words of Yeshua — sell everything you have, give to the poor, and follow me — and he did it. Literally. Immediately. He sold his inheritance, gave the money to the poor, and walked into the Egyptian desert. He stayed there for twenty years. He fought demons. He heard voices. He was beaten and left for dead and Jah healed him. He came out of the desert and people could not explain what they saw in his face. The monks of Egypt gathered around him and he became the father of monasticism — the father of every monk, every friar, every solitary seeker, every Rasta elder on the mountain who has chosen stillness over the noise of Babylon. The teaching he left was simple:

"Whatever you do, do it for the love of Jah and the peace of your soul. Expect to be tempted until your last breath. Keep the eye of the heart open. Flee from the world not with your feet but with your soul."

Saint Moses the Black · 330–405 AD · The Gangster Who Became the Greatest

Moses was Ethiopian. He was a slave who escaped — or was expelled — for violent conduct, and became the leader of a gang of seventy-five thieves that terrorised the Nile delta. He was large, physically powerful, and feared by everyone. Then something happened that Moses himself could never fully explain. He ran from some soldiers and hid in the monastery of Scetis in the Egyptian desert. And the monks received him. No judgment. No rejection. Just: sit down, brother. And in that silence Moses began to change. He went to the desert father Isidore and he learned stillness. He became a monk. He became a priest. He became the most quoted of all the Desert Fathers. When soldiers came to kill him — he knew they were coming, he had seen them in a vision — he told his disciples to go and he stayed. He would not resist. He who had spent his life in violence would not end his life in violence. He was killed in 405 AD, aged 75, with six other monks. His body was found with his arms open.

"Go, sit in your cell. Your cell will teach you everything. Speak no evil. Do not judge. Live the livity and the livity will live you."

"1 am not yet a monk myself. But 1 have seen monks."

Saint Mary of Egypt · 344–421 AD · Forty-Seven Years in the Desert

Mary was Egyptian. At twelve years old she left her family and went to Alexandria and lived in the street. For seventeen years she lived without discipline — she would say it herself, holding nothing back, calling herself what she was. Then at twenty-nine she joined a pilgrimage to Jerusalem for the feast of the Holy Cross. She tried to enter the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and an invisible force stopped her. Four times she tried and four times the door held her back. She went into a corner and wept and looked at an icon of Mary and said: intercede for me. And she felt the force release. She went in, she venerated the cross, and she walked out and crossed the Jordan and went into the desert. She was there for forty-seven years. A monk named Zosimas found her near the end of her life, levitating during prayer. Her body was found incorrupt after death. Her story is in the Ethiopian Orthodox synaxarium. She is one of 1-and-1. The woman who turned around completely and never turned back.

Amma Syncletica · The Desert Mother · Egypt · 4th Century

Amma means mother. And Syncletica was one of the mothers of the desert — a woman who gave up an inheritance, cut her hair, went to a tomb on the edge of Alexandria and lived in it. Women came to her for teaching the way men went to Anthony. And she taught them hard truth: the spiritual path is not peaceful at the beginning. It is war at the beginning. The fire tests you before it purifies you. Babylon does not release without a fight. But those who persist — who go into the furnace and do not run — come out refined. These are her words, speaking direct:

"In the beginning there are a great many battles and a good deal of suffering for those who are advancing toward Jah. But after that there is indescribable joy. It is the same with a fire — it crackles and smokes at first, but then the warmth and the light come through."

Saint Augustine of Hippo · 354–430 AD · The African Who Built Western Theology

Augustine was from Thagaste — modern Algeria — born of a Berber mother named Monica and a Pagan father named Patricius. He was brilliant and restless and spent years pursuing philosophy, pleasure, and every teaching that crossed his path — Manichaeism, Neoplatonism, rhetoric, women. His mother Monica prayed for him for seventeen years without ceasing. Never stopped. Never gave up. And in 386 AD in a garden in Milan, Augustine heard a child's voice saying "take up and read" — he opened Paul's letter to the Romans and the light entered. Everything changed in that moment. He was baptised by Ambrose of Milan. He went back to Africa. He became the Bishop of Hippo — a small North African coastal city — and from that small African city he wrote the theology that shaped the entire Western Christian tradition for sixteen hundred years. The man who wrote it was African. His city was African. His mother was African. Remember who built this.

"Our heart is restless until it rests in Thee. Thou madest us for Thyself, O Jah, and our heart is restless until it repose in Thee."

"Love Jah, and do what you will. For the soul trained in love will do nothing to offend the One it loves."

Saint Monica · 332–387 AD · The Mother Who Never Stopped

Monica was Algerian. Berber. African. And she spent seventeen years praying for her son. Seventeen years of tears, of fasting, of intercession, of not giving up when everything in the natural world said give up. A bishop told her: it is impossible that a son of so many tears should perish. And he did not perish. Augustine became what he became because his mother refused to stop calling his name before Jah. That is the power of a praying African mother. It does not expire. It does not weaken with time. It persists until the answer comes.

Saint Perpetua · 182–203 AD · The African Woman Who Wrote from Prison

Perpetua was North African — from Carthage in modern Tunisia. She was twenty-two years old, nursing a newborn baby, when the Roman government arrested her for being a Christian. She was given many opportunities to renounce her faith and save her life. Her father begged her. The governor begged her. She refused every time. In prison she kept a diary — the oldest surviving Christian text written by a woman. She wrote about her visions, her dreams, her conversations with Jah in that cell. On March 7, 203 AD, she was taken to the arena. She was thrown by a wild heifer, got up, fixed her hair, helped a fellow martyr to his feet, and guided the hand of the inexperienced soldier who was supposed to kill her. Her last words were to her brother: stand firm in the faith, and love one another. She was twenty-two. She had been nursing a baby months before. That is African faith. That is what Babylon could not break.

Saint Josephine Bakhita · 1869–1947 · The Sudanese Woman Who Found Jah in Chains

Bakhita was Sudanese. At the age of seven she was kidnapped by Arab slave traders. They were so brutal that she forgot her own name — Bakhita means fortunate, a name the slave traders gave her in mockery. She passed through five different owners. One of them tattooed her skin with a knife and salt — sixty marks. She was bought by an Italian consul and taken to Italy. There she encountered the Canossian Sisters and heard for the first time the teaching of the God who made her. And something in her recognised it. She said: this is the God 1 have felt since childhood — the one 1 saw in the beauty of nature but did not know the name of. 1 always knew Him. Now 1 know His name. She was baptised. She became a nun. She spent forty-five years in a small Italian town, meeting everyone who came through the door with joy. Children loved her. She would rock them and whisper to them. She died in 1947 saying: Our Lady, Our Lady. She was canonised in 2000. A kidnapped, tattooed, formerly enslaved Sudanese woman is a canonised saint of the universal church. Jah does not waste suffering. He redeems it entirely.

"If 1 were to meet those who kidnapped me and even those who tortured me, 1 would kneel and kiss their hands. For through what they did, Jah led me to Him."

Simon of Cyrene · African · He Who Carried the Cross

Cyrene is in modern Libya. North Africa. Simon of Cyrene was coming in from the fields when the Roman soldiers grabbed him from the crowd on the road to Golgotha and made him carry the cross of Yeshua. An African man carried the cross of Christ. The New Testament records his sons by name — Alexander and Rufus — which means they were known to the early Christian community. Tradition says the touch of that cross changed him. That carrying the weight of the innocent one, even against his will, opened something in him that could not be closed. There is a tradition that identifies Simon with the "Simon called the Black" mentioned in Acts 13 as one of the leaders of the Antioch church — one of the first multiracial Christian communities in history. Whether it is the same man or not, the truth is written in the text: an African was there on the road to Golgotha. An African carried the cross. Africa was present at the central moment of the Christian story.

Saint Martin de Porres · 1579–1639 · The Holy Black · First Black Saint of the Americas

Martin was born in Lima, Peru — the son of a Spanish nobleman and a freed African enslaved woman named Ana Velazquez. His father was ashamed of him because of the colour of his skin and initially refused to register him. Martin grew up in poverty. He became a Dominican lay brother — the lowest rank, because they would not admit him further due to his African heritage. And from that lowest position he became the most beloved figure in Lima. He healed the sick. He fed hundreds of poor people daily. He ran a shelter for the homeless and a hospital for the sick. He bilocated — was seen in two places at once. He levitated during prayer. Rats, dogs, and cats — natural enemies — would eat from the same bowl at his command. He called them all brothers and sisters. Ital livity, Bhakti love, and miraculous power — from the lowest social position in a colonial city. Jah does not choose based on the criteria of Babylon.

Saint Athanasius of Alexandria · 296–373 AD · He Who Stood Against the World

Athanasius was Egyptian. Bishop of Alexandria. And he spent seventeen years in exile — five separate times — because he refused to agree with the heresy of Arius, who said that Yeshua was a created being and not the eternal Son of Jah. The whole empire turned against him. Emperors exiled him. Bishops voted against him. The phrase became: Athanasius against the world. And he held. He returned from exile five times. He outlasted every emperor who exiled him. And the theology he defended — that Jah became flesh so that flesh might become Jah — the doctrine of theosis, of divinization, of the human being raised into divine nature — that theology is the foundation of every mystical tradition in Christianity. It is the Siddha teaching. It is the Vedantic teaching. It is the Rastafari teaching. An Egyptian said it first and held it alone against the world.

"Jah became man so that man might become Jah."

He died before the verdict came. It does not matter. His words survived and they are the same words the Vedic rishis wrote in Sanskrit two thousand years before he was born. Truth speaks itself in every language to every soul that becomes still enough to hear it.

Saint Francis of Assisi · 1181–1226 · Brother Sun · Sister Moon · Ital Love

Francis was the son of a wealthy Italian cloth merchant. He went to war as a young man, was captured, spent a year in prison, came home sick and changed. He began to hear the voice of Jah in broken-down churches, in lepers on the road, in the birds of the field. One day he stripped off every piece of clothing his father had given him, handed it back in the public square, and walked away naked — renouncing in one gesture everything that Babylon had built him with. He went to the birds and preached to them. They listened without flying away. He went to a wolf that was terrorising a village and spoke with it and made peace between the wolf and the people. He received the stigmata — the wounds of Yeshua — in his hands and feet and side, unsought, while in prayer on Mount Alverna. He ate almost nothing and lived with the birds and the poor and called everything his sibling — Brother Fire, Sister Water, Sister Death. He is the Ital saint of the West. The patron of all who know that every creature carries the Prana of Jah.

"Start by doing what is necessary. Then do what is possible. And suddenly you are doing the impossible."

"Lord, make me an instrument of Thy peace. Where there is hatred, let me sow love."

Hildegard of Bingen · 1098–1179 · The Visionary · Healer · Musician · Scientist

Hildegard was a German abbess who from the age of three saw visions of light that she called "the Living Light." She was given everything: she wrote two volumes of visionary theology, she composed over seventy pieces of sacred music — some of the most extraordinary music of the medieval world — she wrote books on medicine and healing that described herbs and foods and their effects on the human body with a precision that reads like Ayurveda. She described the body's healing force as viriditas — green power, life force — the same concept as Prana. She wrote to emperors and popes and told them when they were wrong. She cast out a demon. She preached — a woman preaching publicly in 12th century Europe, which no woman was permitted to do. She did it anyway because Jah told her to and no earthly authority could compete with that instruction. She is the Ayurvedic saint of the West. The Siddha woman in a German monastery.

"The Word is living, sharp, and quick. It works its effects in the human soul. It illumines the darkness and bestows life."

"Holy persons draw to themselves all that is earthly. The earth is at the same time mother — she is the mother of all that is natural and the mother of all that is human."

Julian of Norwich · 1342–1416 · All Shall Be Well

Julian was an English anchoress — she lived in a small room attached to a church in Norwich, with a window to the street and a window to the church. She had asked Jah for three things: to feel the passion of Yeshua in her own body, to receive an illness that would bring her to the edge of death so she could understand how to surrender it, and to receive three wounds — contrition, compassion, and longing for Jah. Jah gave her all three. At thirty years old she became critically ill and in that illness received sixteen revelations — showings — of divine love. She recovered and spent twenty years writing down what she had been shown. She wrote the first book in English ever written by a woman. And the heart of what she was shown was this:

"All shall be well. And all shall be well. And all manner of thing shall be well."

"He said not: thou shalt not be troubled, thou shalt not be travailed, thou shalt not be afflicted. But He said: thou shalt not be overcome."

"In the self-same point that our soul is made sensual, in the self-same point is the City of Jah ordained to Him from without beginning."

Julian called Jah "Mother." In 14th century England. She said that Jah is both Father and Mother — the divine feminine is not a modern invention. It is ancient revelation that the institution buried and the mystics kept alive.

Saint John of the Cross · 1542–1591 · The Dark Night · The Living Flame

Juan de Yepes Álvarez was born in Spain to a poor family. He became a Carmelite monk and joined Teresa of Ávila in reforming the order — bringing it back to its roots of silence, poverty, and intense contemplative prayer. For this he was arrested by his own order, imprisoned in a cell six feet wide, and kept in darkness for nine months. And in that darkness he entered the dark night of the soul — the total stripping away of all consolation, all feeling of Jah's presence, all spiritual experience — until nothing remained but naked faith. And in that nothing he found everything. He wrote his greatest poetry in prison on scraps of paper hidden from the guards. The language of that poetry — the soul as the bride searching for the divine Beloved in the night — is the language of the Bhakti tradition, of the Sufi poets, of the Psalms of David. One fire. Many lamps. John of the Cross is the saint of the Kundalini dark night — the guide for every soul that has felt Jah withdraw and did not know that the withdrawal itself is the teaching.

"In the evening of life we shall be judged on love alone."

"The soul that walks in love neither tires others nor grows tired."
