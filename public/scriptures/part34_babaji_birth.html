From the moment of birth Yeshua radiated what the yogic sciences call the Prema-Pulse — an emanation of unconditional love from the Anahata centre that the fully formed causal body carries as its natural expression. Those who held him as an infant reported an inexplicable peace settling upon them. Animals were calmed in his presence. Children were drawn to him without understanding why. This was not learned behaviour or social skill — it was the natural radiance of a consciousness that had already, in its causal dimension, achieved complete union with Jah.

His mental development was accelerated beyond all normal measure. By age three he was asking questions that reflected the concerns of a soul that had studied the scriptures in many lifetimes. By age six he was spending hours alone in nature — in direct communion with the elemental forces, drawing Prana from the earth and the sun and the wind with the ease of a born yogi. His Mooladhara Nadi was deeply rooted in the primordial ground of being. His Ajna — the centre of higher perception — was already partially open, allowing him to perceive the subtle bodies of those around him and to know, without being told, what was in the heart of the person before him.

By age nine his inner life had deepened to the point where he spent increasing hours in what his parents and neighbours could only call a strange stillness — what the yogic tradition would recognise as the earliest stages of Dharana and Dhyana, the concentration and meditation that precede Samadhi. He was already receiving what might be called Akasha-Neural downloads — direct infusions of knowledge from the causal plane — during these states of inner absorption.

The event at the Temple at age twelve is the one moment the Gospel of Luke preserves from these years, and it is perfectly chosen. The twelve-year-old Yeshua sitting among the teachers of Israel, asking and answering questions with an authority that left them astonished, was the first public eruption of the Christ-Consciousness through the human vessel that had been prepared to carry it. His answer to his anxious mother — Wist ye not that I must be about my Father's business? — was not a child's impertinence. It was the declaration of a soul that had never, for a single moment, lost the awareness of its true identity and its true mission.

His eyes — dark brown also — held something that those who encountered him consistently struggled to describe: a depth that seemed to have no bottom, a quality of seeing that went through the surface of the person before him and perceived something that the person themselves could not quite name but longed to be seen by. This was the perception of the Ajna Nadi operating at full capacity — seeing not the social mask or the karmic personality but the eternal soul within.

His build was that of a man who had walked thousands of miles — lean, strong, weathered by the sun of the Near East and the wind of the Himalayas. His hands were the hands of a carpenter and a traveller. But those who touched those hands described a warmth that went beyond physical temperature — a current of Prana that entered through the touch and left the touched person feeling that something in them had been reorganised at a level they could not articulate.

His voice, when he taught, was described as carrying an authority unlike that of the scribes. This is because the scribes spoke from the outer mind — from memory, from learned tradition, from the intellectual engagement with inherited text. Yeshua spoke from Samadhi — from the state of union with the Source of the scripture. He did not interpret the Torah. He spoke from the place that the Torah was pointing to. This is the difference between a scholar and a Siddha.
