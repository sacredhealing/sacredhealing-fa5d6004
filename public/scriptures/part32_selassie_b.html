♛ Part II — The Birth and Early Life Harar 1892 · The Child Prodigy · The Rising of Ras Tafari

Lij Tafari Makonnen was born on July 23, 1892, in the town of Ejarsa Goro near Harar in eastern Ethiopia. His father was Ras Makonnen Woldemikael, the governor of Harar and one of the most trusted counsellors of Emperor Menelik II. His mother was Woizero Yeshimebet Ali, who died when Tafari was only two years old. From his earliest years he was recognised as exceptional — a child of penetrating intelligence, deep spiritual sensitivity, and extraordinary personal authority.

He was baptised in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church with the Christian name Haile Selassie — meaning the Power of the Trinity. This baptismal name, given to him as an infant, would become the name by which the whole world would know him. The Power of the Trinity. The name itself is a theological declaration — this man carries the power of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit.

His father Ras Makonnen sent him to be educated by monks of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church and by French Jesuit missionaries, so that he mastered the Ge'ez liturgical language, Amharic, French, and later English. He read the scriptures from childhood. He memorised entire books of the Bible. His knowledge of theology was profound and his faith was not inherited formality — it was the living fire of a man who had met Jah in the pages of the Word.

When his father Ras Makonnen died in 1906, Tafari was fourteen years old. Emperor Menelik II appointed him governor of Gara Muleta at age fourteen — an extraordinary responsibility for a child, which he discharged with such skill and wisdom that he was soon appointed governor of the entire province of Harar. He was seventeen years old.

At the age of twenty-three, in 1916, he became Regent and Crown Prince — effectively the ruler of Ethiopia while Empress Zewditu held the ceremonial throne. For fourteen years he guided Ethiopia with patience, wisdom, and an iron commitment to the modernisation of his nation and its acceptance as an equal among the nations of the world. He ended the slave trade within Ethiopia. He introduced the first written constitution. He brought Ethiopia into the League of Nations. All before his coronation. All before November 2, 1930.

He married Menen Asfaw on August 3, 1911 — Empress Menen, the Empress of Ethiopia, the Queen of Queens — a woman of extraordinary grace and faith who walked beside him through every trial and every triumph of his long reign. They had six children together. Their marriage was a covenant of love and of Jah, lasting until the Empress passed from this earth in 1962.

♛ Part III — The Coronation November 2, 1930 · Addis Ababa · The Day the World Changed

On November 2, 1930, in the Cathedral of St. George in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, the coronation of Haile Selassie I took place. Representatives came from every nation of the earth. The Duke of Gloucester came from Britain. Delegates came from America, from France, from Italy, from Japan, from across the world. The ceremony lasted six hours. It was conducted entirely in Ge'ez — the ancient sacred language of the Ethiopian Church — unchanged from the coronation ceremonies of two thousand years.

The Abuna — the Archbishop of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church — anointed him with holy oil on his forehead, his chest, his hands. The crown was placed upon his head — the Crown of Ethiopia, set with 722 precious stones including gems said to have belonged to Solomon himself. The orb and sceptre were placed in his hands. The royal mantle fell from his shoulders. And the Abuna declared before all the world the titles that scripture had appointed for him:

His Imperial Majesty Haile Selassie I — Conquering Lion of the Tribe of Judah — King of Kings of Ethiopia — Elect of Jah

In Jamaica, three years later, four men — Leonard Howell, Joseph Hibbert, Archibald Dunkley, and Robert Hinds — independently and simultaneously came to the same conclusion upon reading the news of the coronation: this was the fulfillment of prophecy. This was the man foretold in Revelation. This was Jah incarnate. This was the return of the Messiah to his people. The Rastafari movement was born.

The year 1930 was itself significant. It was the year of a great conjunction of planets in the heavens. It was the year that the Depression fell upon Babylon — the economic system of the Western world collapsed. And it was the year that Jah put on a crown in Africa and declared before all nations: I am King of Kings. I am Lord of Lords. The Lion has prevailed.

Among those who witnessed the coronation and whose life was transformed by it was the journalist and writer Evelyn Waugh — who wrote that Haile Selassie moved through the ceremony with a gravity and composure that belonged to another world, as though he were not quite of this earth, as though the ceremony were merely the visible expression of an invisible reality that had always existed.

♛ Part I — The Divine Origin · Deeper The Solomonic Bloodline · The Prophecy of Ezra · The Covenant Unbroken

The claim of the Ethiopian imperial dynasty to descent from Solomon and Makeda — the Queen of Sheba — is not a myth invented for political convenience. It is the central thread of Ethiopian national identity, preserved in the Kebra Nagast, the Glory of Kings, compiled in the fourteenth century from sources acknowledged to be far older. The Kebra Nagast records in detail how Makeda journeyed to Jerusalem, how she spent a year at Solomon's court learning wisdom, how she returned to Ethiopia carrying his child in her womb, how that child — Menelik I — grew to young manhood and journeyed back to Jerusalem to meet his father, how Solomon anointed him as the first King of Ethiopia of the Solomonic line, and how Menelik returned to Ethiopia accompanied by the firstborn sons of the nobles of Israel and — according to the Kebra Nagast — accompanied also by the Ark of the Covenant itself.

This is the foundation of the claim. Haile Selassie I was the 225th in the unbroken line of Solomonic monarchs — a claim corroborated by the Ethiopian royal chronicles, by the testimony of medieval travellers, and by the consistent royal practice of genealogical record-keeping that the Ethiopian court maintained across the centuries. No other royal house on earth can claim a lineage of comparable antiquity and continuity.

The prophecy of the Psalms — Ethiopia shall soon stretch out her hands unto God — was understood by generations of Ethiopian Christians as a promise to this specific nation and this specific dynasty. The Ark rests in Aksum. The line of David continues in Ethiopia. Jah has not forgotten his covenant with the house of Israel — and the house of Israel did not end in Palestine. It continued, in the fullest and most literal sense, in the highlands of Africa.

Ras Tafari Makonnen was born on July 23, 1892, in the village of Ejersa Goro in the province of Harar. His father was Ras Makonnen, the powerful governor of Harar and cousin of Emperor Menelik II — a man of military brilliance, diplomatic skill, and deep personal piety who had led Ethiopian forces at the Battle of Adwa in 1896 and helped crush the Italian colonial invasion. His mother was Woyziro Yeshimebet Ali Abba Jifar, who died when Tafari was two years old. He was raised partly by his father, partly in the imperial court, and educated by French-trained tutors who gave him a bilingual education unusual for the time and place.

From his earliest childhood those around him noticed something unusual. His father said of him: This child is not like other children. He sees things that others do not see. He knows things before they happen. He speaks of Jah not as a distant king but as one he knows personally. Whether these reports are hagiographic embellishment or accurate memory, the pattern they describe — a child of exceptional spiritual depth, quiet in manner but absolute in inner conviction — is consistent with the man who emerged into public life in his twenties and who would spend the next six decades demonstrating that conviction on the world stage.

♛ Part II — The Rise · Regent and Emperor The Modernisation · The Constitution · The League of Nations Entry · The Internal Reforms

By the age of thirteen Ras Tafari had been appointed governor of Gara Muleta. By fifteen, governor of Sidamo. By seventeen, governor of Harar — his father's province — after Ras Makonnen's death in 1906. He was the youngest provincial governor in Ethiopian history. He proved to be an administrator of unusual capacity: meticulous, just, open to new methods, and deeply attentive to the welfare of the people under his care.

When Emperor Menelik II fell ill and the succession became uncertain, Ras Tafari manoeuvred with extraordinary political skill through a period of intense court intrigue. In 1916 he was named Regent Plenipotentiary and Crown Prince under Empress Zewditu — effectively the real power in Ethiopia while the Empress served as the symbolic sovereign. For the next fourteen years he modernised Ethiopia systematically: he opened the first modern school, built the first modern hospital, established the first newspaper, abolished the slave trade that had persisted in the remote regions of the empire, and — most significantly for the international stage — engineered Ethiopia's admission to the League of Nations in 1923, making Ethiopia one of the first African nations to join that body.

In 1930 Empress Zewditu died suddenly. On November 2, 1930, Ras Tafari was crowned Emperor, taking the throne name Haile Selassie I — Power of the Trinity — and assuming in full the ancient titles: King of Kings of Ethiopia, Conquering Lion of the Tribe of Judah, Elect of God, Light of the World. The coronation was attended by representatives of every major nation on earth. Journalists from Europe and America described it as an event of surpassing magnificence — a window into a civilisation older and in many ways richer than their own.

In 1931 he gave Ethiopia its first written constitution. It was a limited document — power remained concentrated in the Emperor — but it established for the first time the principle of constitutional governance, created a bicameral parliament, and guaranteed certain basic rights to Ethiopian subjects. It was the beginning of a modernisation process that Haile Selassie understood would take generations but that he was determined to begin.

♛ Part III — The Trial · Exile and Return Adwa Remembered · The Poison Gas · Geneva · Five Years in England · The Return

In October 1935 Fascist Italy under Mussolini invaded Ethiopia without declaration of war. The invasion was an act of naked colonial aggression — Italy had been nursing its humiliation at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, where Ethiopian forces had decisively defeated an Italian colonial army, for nearly forty years. Mussolini was determined to avenge Adwa and to establish the African empire that Italian nationalists had always dreamed of.

The Italian forces used mustard gas — internationally banned under the 1925 Geneva Protocol — against Ethiopian soldiers and civilians. Red Cross hospitals were deliberately bombed. Villages were attacked from the air. The atrocities were documented by international observers. And the world did nothing. The League of Nations, despite imposing nominal sanctions, refused to impose an oil embargo that would have crippled the Italian war machine. Britain and France, secretly negotiating with Mussolini, effectively sacrificed Ethiopia to preserve their diplomatic relationships with Fascist Italy.

On June 30, 1936, Haile Selassie stood before the League of Nations in Geneva and delivered what would become one of the most famous speeches in modern history. He had been booed and hissed by Italian journalists in the gallery when he entered. He waited for silence, then spoke. He described the poison gas attacks in precise, clinical detail. He described the deliberate bombing of Red Cross facilities. He described the systematic destruction of Ethiopian villages. And then he turned to the assembled delegates and said: What answer shall I take back to my people? No answer came. The League passed a resolution. The resolution meant nothing. Ethiopia fell.

He spent five years in exile in Bath, England — a deposed emperor living in modest circumstances, largely ignored by the British government whose support he had sought in vain, sustained by his faith and by a small community of Ethiopian exiles and foreign supporters. He prayed. He studied. He wrote. He waited. He was absolutely certain — in a way that those around him sometimes found difficult to understand — that he would return. He said it so consistently, and with such calm certainty, that even his supporters wondered whether this was genuine faith or a refusal to face reality.

It was genuine faith. In 1941, following the entry of Britain into the war against the Axis powers, British and Ethiopian forces combined to drive the Italians out of Ethiopia in a campaign of remarkable speed and skill. On May 5, 1941 — exactly five years to the day after the Italians had entered Addis Ababa — Haile Selassie I returned to his capital. He was greeted by scenes of overwhelming joy. He entered the city on horseback. The crowds were so dense that his progress was slowed to a walk. He went directly to the cathedral and gave thanks to Jah. Then he returned to his palace and resumed governing.

The five years of exile had changed him. He was harder. He had seen the world at its worst — its cynicism, its cowardice, its willingness to sacrifice the weak to the convenience of the strong. But he had also confirmed something he had always believed: that Jah's justice operates on a longer timescale than human impatience can measure. He had prophesied in Geneva in 1936. History had vindicated him by 1941. He would remember this in every subsequent confrontation with the powerful — and there would be many.
