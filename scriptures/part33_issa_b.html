Yeshua in the East · The Lost Years · Part 2

The Unknown Years · Luke 2:52 to Luke 3:23 · Eighteen Years of Silence · The Notovitch Manuscript

The second part of the Issa Nath transmission — the eighteen missing years of Yeshua's life. The jjourney through India, Persia, Egypt. The Notovitch manuscript. The Buddhist monasteries. The formation of the Christ-Consciousness through the living traditions of the East.

☸ Part 1 — The Unknown Years · The Missing Eighteen Luke 2:52 to Luke 3:23 · Eighteen Years of Silence

The Gospel of Luke records one moment from the childhood of Yeshua. He is twelve years old. His parents bring him to Jerusalem for Passover. On the return jjourney they discover he is missing. After three days of searching they find him in the Temple, sitting among the teachers, asking and answering questions that leave the wisest men of Israel astonished. His mother says to him: Son, why have you done this to us? And he answers: Did you not know that 1 must be about my Father's business?

Then Luke writes eighteen words that have puzzled every reader for two thousand years: And he went down with them and came to Nazareth and was subject unto them. And Yeshua increased in wisdom and stature and in fayour with Jah and man. And then — silence. The next time Yeshua appears in any Gospel he is approximately thirty years old, being baptized in the Jordan by John. Eighteen years vanish from the record.

The question that has haunted Christianity for two millennia is simple: Where was he? What happened between age twelve and age thirty? What transformed the carpenter's son from Nazareth into the most powerful spiritual teacher the world has ever produced — a man who could heal the sick, raise the dead, command the wind and the sea, and speak with an authority that left even his enemies speechless?

The answer preserved in the monasteries of the East is this: He went to India. He travelled the ancient trade routes of the Silk Road — routes well established in the first century, regularly used by Jewish merchants, Essene travellers, and seekers of wisdom. He went to learn. He went to complete what had begun in the Temple at age twelve. He went to receive from the ancient wisdom traditions of the East what no rabbi in Judaea could give him.

The documentary evidence for this jjourney exists in multiple independent sources — a Tibetan manuscript discovered in 1887, Sufi traditions going back to the first century, Kashmiri oral histories preserved by the Nath yogis, and the testimony of the masters and monasteries he visited, whose names and locations are recorded in the Eastern traditions with extraordinary specificity. What follows is the complete account.

☸ Part 2 — The Notovitch Manuscript The Himis Monastery · Ladakh · 1887 · The Unknown Life of Yeshua

In 1887, a Russian journalist and traveller named Nicolas Notovitch broke his leg near the Himis monastery in Ladakh — the high Tibetan Buddhist monastery in the Himalayas of northern India. While recovering, the chief lama showed him two ancient manuscript rolls written in Tibetan — a translation, the lama explained, of a much older Pali document preserved in Lhasa. The document was titled: The Life of Saint Issa, Best of the Sons of Men.

Notovitch published the translation in 1894 under the title The Unknown Life of Yeshua. The manuscript caused a global sensation and equally fierce condemnation from the institutional church. But its contents — independently corroborated by later researchers including Swami Abhedananda in 1922 and Nicholas Roerich in 1925, who both visited Himis and saw the same manuscript — tell a story that cannot be dismissed.

The manuscript records that at the age of thirteen or fourteen, a young Israelite named Issa left the land of Israel and joined a merchant caravan travelling east. He passed through Sindh — the Indus Valley — and settled for a time among the Aryans who received him with honour. He studied the Vedas under the Brahmin priests of Jagannath in the holy city of Puri in Orissa. He studied the laws of Manu. He learned Sanskrit. He read the Upanishads and the Vedic hymns. He sat with the wisest teachers of Hinduism and absorbed the deepest teachings of the ancient tradition.

But Issa was troubled by the caste system he found in India — the division of humanity into Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Shudra — with the lowest castes, the Sudras and the untouchables, denied access to the sacred texts and the temples of Jah. He preached openly against caste — declaring that Jah makes no distinction between his children, that the soul of the Brahmin and the soul of the untouchable are equal before the Almighty, that the temple of Jah is the human heart and no earthly priest can close it to any man.

This teaching made the Brahmin priests his enemies. They plotted against him. He was forced to flee Puri and travelled north — through the sacred city of Rajagriha, through Benares, through the forests of the Himalayan foothills — seeking the Buddhist communities of the north, where he found a more receptive audience for his teaching of universal love and the equality of all souls before the divine.

☸ Part 3 — Among the Buddhists of the Himalayas Nepal · Tibet · The Pali Scriptures · Six Years of Study

Issa spent six years among the Buddhist communities of Nepal and the Himalayan foothills. He studied the Pali canon — the sutras and sayings of Gautama Buddha. He sat in deep meditation in the monasteries of the mountains. He learned the principles of dharma — right action, right thought, right speech. He absorbed the teaching of ahimsa — non-violence, compassion for all living creatures — which would become the foundation of his own teaching: love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, turn the other cheek.

The Buddhist teachers recognised in Issa a being of exceptional spiritual attainment. In the tradition: manuscript, the lamas declared that he had descended from the Absolute, that he embodied the divine spark more fully than any man they had encountered, and that he had been chosen to remind humanity of the one Jah and to unify the human family in the brotherhood of love.

During these years Issa also encountered the Jain tradition — the ancient Indian philosophy of complete non-violence and the equality of all souls — and the Zoroastrian fire-worshippers of Persia, whose teaching of the cosmic battle between light and darkness, Ahura Mazda and Ahriman, would echo in his later teaching about the kingdom of light and the powers of darkness.

He studied astronomy. He studied the movements of the planets and the stars. The Magi who came from the East to his birth were astrologers — men of the same tradition he now studied. The star they had followed was not accidental. The heavens had been announcing his coming, and in the East they had known how to read the announcement.

He studied healing. Ayurveda — the ancient Indian science of life — taught him the healing properties of herbs, roots, and natural medicines. He learned the science of prana — the life force that flows through all living bodies — and how to direct it through touch, through word, through the focused intention of a fully awakened consciousness. The healings he performed on his return to Palestine were not miracles of the supernatural. They were the natural expression of a consciousness so fully aligned with Jah that the laws of disease and death yielded before it.
